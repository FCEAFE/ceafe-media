## Helsing scales back its Plymouth drone plant after losing the Royal Navy's Atlantic Net to a British rival

**Helsing, the German defence start-up described by Bloomberg as Europe's most valuable, is retreating from its flagship UK project to build underwater naval drones, Bloomberg reported on 18 November, citing people familiar with the matter.**

Navy Lookout reports that Helsing has made 10 staff redundant in Plymouth and is moving research and development closer to its London software hub, while keeping some presence in the city. The trigger was the Royal Navy's choice, on 10 November, of Hampshire-based Ocean Infinity Solutions as commercial mission partner for Atlantic Net, a £90 million framework of uncrewed vehicles, sensors and AI for North Atlantic anti-submarine surveillance; Helsing had bid in a consortium with QinetiQ. Helsing says it remains on course to invest £350 million in the UK, the pledge it made under the 2024 Anglo-German Trinity House agreement. The US firm Anduril, which also pitched for Atlantic Bastion, posted overnight test footage of its Seabed Sentry sensor on the floor of a Scottish loch; the UK has said no Seabed Sentry contract has been placed.

**Why it matters for Europe.** London chose a domestic supplier over both a German and a US bidder, which keeps the capability, jobs and data in the UK. But the case also shows how fragmented Europe's defence market remains: a European champion that opened a factory with ministerial fanfare a year ago gets no order, and its capacity moves elsewhere. A European market with common demand (EDIP, SAFE, joint naval programmes) would let firms like Helsing and Ocean Infinity scale on European orders instead of winning or losing national contests one by one. If Europe wants to compete on equal terms, it has to pool what is today fragmented.

**What to watch.** Further Atlantic Bastion tenders; whether Helsing's maritime work (naval HX-2, Contra) wins UK or German orders; Helsing's role in the Palantir-alternatives debate.

## Update: NATO's space-data marketplace runs on a US Space Force platform

**NATO and the US Space Force launched NATO's Global Data Marketplace on 17 November at NATO EDGE 26 in İzmir, to buy commercial space data, products and services, starting with space domain awareness.**

New details published overnight show the procurement platform is the US Space Force Joint Commercial Operations solution from Bluestaq, a Colorado-based company that the Space Force has used since 2020. NATO says 222 companies from 17 Allied nations have already joined through its SPACENET industry group, and first orders, for the Space Theatre Component at Allied Air Command in Ramstein, are expected before the end of 2026. Purchased data goes into a NATO Unified Data Library; UK Defence Journal reports that both are hosted on Amazon Web Services' European sovereign cloud and that NATO keeps ownership of the data it buys.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The marketplace opens a door for European space start-ups to sell to the Alliance, which is welcome. But the front door itself is a US platform, the cloud is a US hyperscaler's "sovereign" offer, and the model is copied from the Space Force. Europe already funds its own space-surveillance capacity (EU SST) and a sovereign constellation (IRIS²); without a European procurement layer and European infrastructure, European firms' data reaches commanders through American systems. Without a common industrial, technological and defence base, Europe will keep depending on decisions taken in Washington.

**What to watch.** The first orders and which countries' firms win them; earlier coverage: HawkEye 360's GDM role via Luxembourg; NATO Starlift launch programme; also in today's Conflictos brief).

## MaiaSpace signs its first commercial customer: Luxembourg's Space Cargo Unlimited

**MaiaSpace, the ArianeGroup subsidiary developing Maia, a small launcher with a reusable first stage, announced on 18 November that Space Cargo Unlimited has bought launch capacity on its first commercial mission.**

Space Cargo Unlimited, headquartered in Luxembourg, will fly its BentoBox in-space manufacturing platform. Aviation Week, interviewing the customer at Space Tech Expo Europe in Bremen, puts the launch "likely in 2028", after Maia's demonstration flights; the inaugural mission is due in 2027. Maia, launched from the Guiana Space Centre, is designed for up to 4 tonnes to low Earth orbit depending on version and kick stage. Space Cargo Unlimited is co-developing its own REV1 return vehicle with Thales Alenia Space for 2030, but has also booked flights on ATMOS Phoenix 2.1 (2027) and SpaceX's Starfall (no earlier than 2028).

**Why it matters for Europe.** A European customer buying a European launch, on a European reusable rocket, is exactly the value chain Europe needs if it wants independent access to space. The customer's parallel SpaceX booking is a reminder that European demand will go to US providers unless European launchers fly soon and reliably. Europe has the resources; what it lacks, time and again, is the scale that only joint action can provide, here meaning firm European institutional launch demand.

**What to watch.** Prometheus engine tests at Vernon (H1 2027); European launcher challenge; PLD Space's first commercial customer (same brief).

## US charges a Russian national with spying on Palantir employees

**A US federal grand jury has indicted Igor D'iu, a Russian national, on charges of conspiring to act and acting as an agent of a foreign government, interstate stalking and identity-document fraud, the Justice Department said on 18 November.**

Prosecutors say Telegram handlers told him his work was for "the Motherland" and directed him to gather information on a Palo Alto technology and defence company because it supplied weapons used against Russia in Ukraine. The indictment does not name the company; NBC News, citing three people familiar with the investigation, and Reuters identify it as Palantir. According to the affidavit, D'iu entered the US from Mexico in mid-2026, photographed employees' laptop screens, positioned laptops for remote access and followed one employee for nearly 30 miles. NBC reports no Palantir systems were breached.

**Why it matters for Europe.** European defence firms face the same threat, as the attempted arson at Estonia's Threod Systems showed. Industrial security of the defence supply chain (staff, sites, data) should be part of a common European approach, not left to each capital. The case also underlines how central US software firms have become to Ukraine's war, a dependence Europe is only starting to address with its own alternatives.

**What to watch.** 24 Nov hearing; any Russian official response; European parallels (Threod Systems, earlier sabotage cases).

## Senate's 2027 bill: a much bigger Space Force budget and more national-security launches

**The Senate Appropriations Committee's draft 2027 defence bill would give the Space Force $54.7 billion, up 73% from $31.6 billion this year (Air & Space Forces Magazine's framing; its table shows $26.1 billion enacted for 2026 excluding reconciliation), though below the Pentagon's $59.1 billion base request.**

National Security Space Launch would get $7.08 billion against a $4.12 billion request, raising planned launches from 31 to 54. Golden Dome gets $9.4 billion, about half the $17.5 billion requested; the Space Development Agency's missile-tracking satellites are cut from $2.73 billion to $833 million, while Next-Gen OPIR Polar, which the Pentagon wanted to cancel, gets $520 million. Breaking Defense quotes the House Armed Services Committee chair saying the $350 billion reconciliation bill is "not going to happen".

**Why it matters for Europe.** One US committee adds more money to military launch in a single year than many European space budgets hold in total, and 54 national-security launches is a demand base that no European launcher has. Appropriators also told the Space Force to keep "multiple vendors" instead of sole-source awards: a reminder that the US funds competition inside its own industry while it asks Europe to open its market to US suppliers.

**What to watch.** Earlier Senate cuts to fighters; Vulcan return to flight.
