Update: US LNG for Slovakia and Hungary through Poland, signed at P-TEC in Warsaw

At the seventh Partnership for Transatlantic Energy Cooperation (P-TEC) in Warsaw, US Energy Secretary Chris Wright announced that “today’s agreements between @GrupaORLEN, Slovakia, and Hungary will help bring more American LNG into Central Europe through Poland”. The company documents are more cautious and give no volumes: ORLEN and Slovakia’s SPP signed an agreement that “opens a new phase of talks on gas supplies using LNG imported into Poland”, and ORLEN and Hungary’s MVM ONEnergy signed a memorandum on possible deliveries via Polish terminals (ORLEN; PAP). ORLEN and Naftogaz also signed a contract for three partial cargoes of US LNG for Ukraine in the first quarter of 2027, implementing a September memorandum. At an Atlantic Council event Wright said Western European countries “have lost their way” and that “the economic and energy future of Europe will not be written in Paris or London” but in Warsaw, Prague, Bratislava, Sofia, Bucharest and Athens.

The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) records that Europe took a record 10.3 billion cubic feet a day of US LNG in 2025, 68% of US LNG exports (34% in 2021); the EU energy regulator ACER says the US supplied 58% of the EU’s LNG imports in 2025. The chart by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, covered at 07:30, puts European gas at about 11 times the Henry Hub price.

Why it matters for Europe. Europe keeps raising its US energy purchases, so the goal of ending dependence on a single supplier has failed: Russia has been replaced by the United States, at a much higher price. At that gap European industry cannot compete. Volkswagen’s CFO said four German plants have no economically viable follow-up production after the early 2030s (Reuters), Mercedes-Benz has halted new investment in its German plants (Reuters, 12 November), and British Steel survives only in public ownership since 16 July 2026 (GOV.UK); its workers’ union assessment put energy prices at the centre of its viability. Wright’s list of capitals, without Paris, deals with Europe one capital at a time rather than with the Union.

Figures that illustrate, once again, why European sovereignty is only credible if it is exercised together.

What to watch. Whether the SPP and MVM memoranda become contracts with volumes; the P-TEC ministerial on 19 November (long-term LNG, civil nuclear).

Sources: @SecretaryWright (post), ORLEN, PAP, Naftogaz, EIA, ACER, GSM Global, Reuters (Volkswagen), Reuters via Euronext (Mercedes-Benz), GOV.UK (British Steel).

US congressional commission calls for a single “economic statecraft” agency and holds up Dutch EUV controls as the model

The US-China Economic and Security Review Commission (USCC) released its 2026 Annual Report on 18 November with 30 recommendations. It says the People’s Liberation Army is “actively soliciting” Nvidia’s most advanced chips for AI-enabled weapons and that “China is seeking to evade U.S. export controls”, including through remote access to data centres in third countries such as Malaysia. It recommends a consolidated entity grouping BIS, OFAC, the State Department’s export-control office and the Pentagon’s DTSA, with Treasury-style enforcement powers; moving China licence reviews from a “presumption of denial” to a “policy of denial”; and requiring AI-chip exporters to certify, under criminal penalties, that sales to China do not divert US capacity. It names ASML as the “gatekeeper” of EUV lithography and cites the US–Netherlands–Japan coordinated controls as “the strongest piece of evidence in support of allied cooperation”. The same chapter notes that Beijing granted European firms fewer general licences for gallium, germanium and graphite than pledged. China’s response: no MOFCOM or MFA statement on the report yet; Global Times argued that China’s AI build-out rests on domestic chips.

Why it matters for Europe. A European monopoly is used as an instrument of US policy, with no European say over the target and no reciprocal leverage, while Europeans pay for Chinese counter-measures to controls decided in Washington. A US agency with Treasury-style powers would make that extraterritorial reach stronger still.

We see once again how a power with global reach operates at a scale that no European state can match on its own.

What to watch. MOFCOM/MFA reaction; whether Congress takes up the export-control amendment; the Trump–Xi talks in Shenzhen.

Sources: USCC (executive summary), USCC (chapter 8), Reuters via Investing.com, CNBC, South China Morning Post, Global Times (post).

US ambassador Puzder wants EU climate and due-diligence rules cut under the Turnberry deal

US Ambassador to the EU Andrew Puzder, welcoming the US Chamber of Commerce to Brussels, wrote that “there’s still work to be done to uphold the historic Turnberry Agreement, because non-tariff barriers are effectively tariffs by another name”. At a Fórum Europa event the day before he named the targets: the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, the deforestation regulation, a three-year delay to the methane regulation for oil and gas importers, and the reach of the CSRD and CSDDD directives over US companies, which he called “not acceptable”. He would not say whether the US will cut its tariffs on steel and aluminium derivatives to 15% before the 31 December deadline, after which the Commission may suspend tariff preferences for the US.

Why it matters for Europe. The EU has already delivered the tariff side of Turnberry; Washington now asks it to change its own laws to suit US exporters, while keeping its own tariffs on steel and aluminium derivatives and its own extraterritorial rules.

What to watch. The 31 December steel and aluminium deadline; the Commission’s answer on CBAM and the methane rule.

Sources: @USAmbEU (post), Fórum Europa, MLex, US Mission to the EU.

China and South Korea complete the second phase of their free-trade agreement

China’s Ministry of Commerce announced on 18 November that the second phase of the China–South Korea FTA has been “substantially concluded” after more than eight years and 17 formal rounds. It covers investment, services, financial services and economic cooperation, with negative-list market access; the text now goes to legal review before signing. MOFCOM calls it a “concrete action” against unilateralism and protectionism. Xi Jinping and Lee Jae-myung met in Shenzhen, and Lee told the APEC CEO Summit that “even as trade barriers rise, we must continue to open up”. Chinese customs data reported by Global Times show China’s trade with other APEC economies up 25.4% year on year to 27.08 trillion yuan ($4.06 trillion) in January–October 2026.

Why it matters for Europe. A close US ally is deepening its opening to China on its own terms, while the EU–China Comprehensive Agreement on Investment, concluded in principle in December 2020, remains frozen. Washington’s Asian partners keep their options open with Beijing; Europe, with a larger market than South Korea, has chosen to align.

What to watch. Signing date and published text.

Sources: MOFCOM, Global Times (Chinese state media), China MFA, South Korea MOTIR.

Brussels proposes an “Advanced Materials for Europe” agenda and admits European IP is draining abroad through foreign acquisitions

The European Commission proposed on 18 November a Council Recommendation to make the EU the world’s leading innovator, producer and user of advanced materials by 2035, with three benchmarks: at least 20% more research investment, 20% more EU-based start-ups and scale-ups, and 20% of projects reaching higher deployment levels. Commissioner Ekaterina Zaharieva proposed at least five joint “priority challenges”, for example permanent magnets without rare earths and substitutes for PFAS in chips. The proposal’s explanatory text cites “technology leakage risks and the drain of European intellectual property (IP) in this sector due to foreign licensing or acquisitions of EU IP holders”. EUobserver notes that the targets are voluntary.

Why it matters for Europe. The diagnosis matches CEAFE’s: European-funded technology leaves through licensing and takeovers. The remedy is non-binding, with no screening of foreign purchases of EU IP holders, no ownership-retention clause and no mandatory European preference: the tools the US uses as a matter of course (CFIUS, ITAR, Buy American).

What to watch. Council discussion; any link to the revision of the FDI Screening Regulation and the Critical Raw Materials Act.

Sources: European Commission (via CNA), European Commission (draft Recommendation), EUobserver, @EZaharievaEU (post).

ECB warns of AI-valuation and sovereign-debt risks, as Apollo says Europe needs €1.4 trillion a year

The ECB’s November Financial Stability Review says risks “remain elevated, given persistent geoeconomic uncertainty and growing exposure to AI”: AI valuations rely on “ambitious earnings expectations”, AI infrastructure is increasingly debt-funded, and fiscal imbalances in major economies could trigger a global repricing of sovereign risk. When volatility rose in March 2026, hedge funds cut euro-area repo positions by 13%, reducing net borrowing by about €50 billion, especially against Spanish and Italian collateral. The same day Apollo’s head of Europe, Diego De Giorgi, told Bloomberg that Europe needs about €1.4 trillion of investment a year for AI, defence and power, above Mario Draghi’s €800 billion estimate, and that its capital markets are not deep enough to supply it.

Why it matters for Europe. Without a savings and investments union, which the ECB again calls “essential”, the gap is filled by US asset managers and private-credit funds, which then shape what gets financed and keep the returns.

What to watch. The ECB’s December meeting; progress on the savings and investments union.

Sources: ECB, ECB (FSR box), Bloomberg (post), Quartz.
