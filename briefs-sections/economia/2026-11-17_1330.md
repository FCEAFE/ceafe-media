## Update: Washington extends its chip controls to data centres worldwide, and Beijing calls it “economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction”

**On 16 November the US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) issued an interim final rule on remote access to advanced “super intelligence” (SI) chips and servers.**

BIS says the rule was “written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council” and that it is meant to stop existing restrictions from being circumvented “through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries”. The rule adds a new end-use control: a licence is required to ship advanced chips and servers to a data centre **anywhere in the world** that provides more than 12 million “aggregate TPP” of compute to companies headquartered (or whose ultimate parent is headquartered) in China, another Country Group D:5 destination or Macau. That means European data centres are covered too, if they serve such customers. Data centres already serving those customers must obtain a licence within 90 days or “face future export restrictions”, and exports of advanced chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil now need a licence because of a “higher-than-normal risk of diversion” to China. The same day, White House science and technology director Michael Kratsios announced the carrot that goes with the stick: Commerce designated 33 preset consortia under the “American SI Exports Program”, full-stack US AI packages backed by priority licensing, advocacy and financing from Commerce, State, EXIM, DFC, USTDA, Treasury, USTR and SBA, so that “U.S. technology [is] the easiest, most attractive choice for allies and partners”. @F_CEAFE reposted the House China Select Committee’s announcement, Kratsios’s post, the Commerce–Justice statement on the Megaspeed probe, an analyst’s thread on the rule and, at 11:09 Madrid, the Chinese Foreign Ministry’s response.

**China’s reaction (linked):** At the MFA’s regular press conference on 17 November, spokesperson Guo Jiakun said: “The move is yet another case of the U.S. overstretching the concept of national security, and abusing export control measures to interfere in normal trade and tech cooperation between China and other countries and go after China’s high-tech industry. Such typical economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction run counter to the common understandings between the two countries, harm the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese companies, and disrupt global supply chains. China will do what is necessary to firmly safeguard its legitimate and lawful rights and interests.” Asked about Megaspeed, he said China’s position on US chip exports to China “is consistent”. On MOFCOM’s Spokesman’s Remarks page, the latest item at about 11:45 Madrid was still the 13 November statement on the Unreliable Entity List, so the ministry had not issued a separate statement yet. Global Times (Chinese state media) and CGTN (Chinese state media) carried the MFA line, and CGTN framed it as China accusing the US of “economic coercion and overreach”.

**Why it matters for Europe.** This is an extraterritorial rule in the full sense. A US agency, acting on Treasury and NSC instructions, now decides which customers a data centre in Frankfurt, Dublin or Madrid may serve if it runs American chips. The rule removes the de minimis exemption for this end use and expects data centres to hand their “know your customer” checks to exporters, where a refusal counts as a red flag. Europe has no chip of its own in this class: the continent’s fastest supercomputer, JUPITER, runs on NVIDIA GH200. EU cloud and AI providers therefore follow Washington’s China policy whether or not the EU has agreed to it, just as Europeans already did on Iranian and Venezuelan oil, Huawei in 5G networks and the frozen EU–China investment agreement (CAI). The “American SI Exports Program” finishes the picture: allies are offered subsidised US stacks on condition of “trusted” use. Beijing’s warning of “necessary measures” could land on European firms caught in the middle, as the Nexperia case showed. Here is another example of why Europeans need to act together: only a common European compute and chip base would let Europe decide its own export rules.

**What to watch.** Federal Register publication (19 Nov) and the start of the 90-day clock; whether MOFCOM announces countermeasures (Unreliable Entity List, rare-earth or other export controls); whether the Commission or the Dutch, Irish and German governments say anything on the rule’s reach into EU data centres; the Trump–Xi meeting in Shenzhen (19 Nov) and the US–China truce that runs to 10 January. Earlier coverage: 16 Nov 07:30–20:30 and 17 Nov 07:30.

## Megaspeed: Washington details a chip-smuggling dragnet that runs through Singapore

**The US Commerce and Justice departments released a joint statement on their investigation of Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based cloud-computing company and Nvidia customer.**

According to the statement as reported by CNBC and SCMP, Commerce began investigating Megaspeed in 2024 for possible export-control violations and asked Nvidia for its sales records in May 2025; Nvidia put Megaspeed on its “no sell” list in June 2025, and the Justice Department then took over the criminal investigation. The statement also says 17 defendants in six separate investigations have been charged with smuggling billions of dollars’ worth of semiconductors. Vice President JD Vance said the administration was concerned about any reports that a third country had obtained Nvidia technology (Reuters post). Singapore is now seeking talks with Washington on the AI chip rules (SCMP). Separately, Reuters reported in an exclusive that Chinese entities are using increasingly sophisticated ways to use output from leading American AI models to train domestic rivals as chip curbs bite.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The probe is the enforcement arm of the rule in topic 1. Third-country hubs like Singapore now carry the burden of proving their customers are clean, which is exactly what European data centres will be asked to do. A European AI industry that depends on US chips also inherits US investigations, “no sell” lists and the legal risk Nvidia is now passing on to its buyers (CNBC via Reuters post).

**What to watch.** charges or Entity List designations tied to Megaspeed; Singapore–US talks; Nvidia’s results and its new export-control board committee.

## China’s envoy in Madrid: Europe should “not follow the instructions of others” on ASML chip-tool exports

**In an interview with Reuters in Madrid, Qu Xun, China’s minister-counsellor in Spain, said long-standing Dutch limits on exporting ASML chipmaking equipment to China are casting a shadow over EU–China industrial-policy talks.**

“We want the European Union to make its own decisions and not follow the instructions of others,” he said, adding: “We want to import ASML goods.” He warned that “Europe is forbidding it because it is afraid China will rob its technology… If Europe thinks that way, China will too”, implying that China could limit the sharing of its own technology. Reuters notes that the Netherlands has restricted ASML exports to China since 2019 and that the US is pushing for curbs on lower-level machines, with stricter controls under discussion in Congress that would also hit Japan’s Tokyo Electron. Qu said China seeks “equality and reciprocity” as the EU weighs local-content, technology-transfer and ownership conditions on foreign investors in EVs, batteries and cars. He also said Chinese investment in Spain has tripled in a decade to €12 billion, with SAIC, Chery, CATL and AESC among the investors.

**Why it matters for Europe.** This is the other side of topic 1. ASML is Europe’s one undisputed world chokepoint in semiconductors, and its exports to China are set by Dutch licences shaped by US pressure. Beijing is now openly linking that alignment to Europe’s access to Chinese battery and EV technology, which is exactly what Spain is trying to attract. CEAFE’s point is that Europe should decide its export policy as a bloc, on its own interests, rather than member by member under pressure from either Washington or Beijing.

**What to watch.** any Dutch or Commission reply; US congressional moves on chip-tool controls; MOFCOM’s line on EU investment conditions.

## TOP500: China keeps the world’s fastest supercomputer on a fully Chinese stack, while Europe’s best runs on American chips

**The 68th TOP500 list (November 2026, announced at SC26 on 16 November) keeps China’s LineShine at No.**

1 with 2.198 exaflop/s on the HPL benchmark, about 21% ahead of the US El Capitan (1.809 EF/s). LineShine, at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, uses the custom “LingKun” platform with 304-core LX2 processors, the proprietary LingQi interconnect and Kylin OS, and it also leads the HPCG ranking (22.00 PF/s). It is the first China-based No. 1 since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017. The US holds Nos. 2–4 (El Capitan, Frontier, Aurora). Europe’s best is the EuroHPC JUPITER Booster at Jülich (Germany), No. 5 and Europe’s first exascale system at exactly 1.000 EF/s, built by Eviden on NVIDIA GH200 Grace Hopper superchips. Eni’s HPC7 and HPC6 (Italy) are Nos. 6 and 8 (HPE/AMD), and Switzerland’s Alps is No. 10. By country, CEAFE’s count from the official workbook gives the US 163 systems (32.6%) and 37.6% of total HPL performance, the EU-27 together 137 systems (27.4%) and 24.0%, and China 18 systems (3.6%) but 12.0% of performance. Germany alone has 47 systems (7.3%), Italy 20 (7.7%) and France 20 (1.6%). Global Times (Chinese state media) highlighted the result, and @F_CEAFE reposted it.

{{chart: top500}}

**Why it matters for Europe.** Measured together, the EU-27 is a real second pole in supercomputing; measured country by country, no member state comes close to the US or China. Europe’s flagship machine depends on US processors, the very kind of hardware whose use Washington now regulates worldwide, whereas China has built its No. 1 on its own chips, interconnect and operating system. The European Processor Initiative and EuroHPC exist precisely to close that gap. The EU as a whole can bear comparison; each capital on its own cannot. That is the difference that matters.

**What to watch.** next list June 2027; EuroHPC’s next exascale system (Alice Recoque, France). Rankings file updated.

## China’s Sinochem cuts its Pirelli stake to 13.6% after Italy’s “golden power” curbs

**Marco Polo International Italy, the vehicle of Chinese state-owned Sinochem (CNRC), sold 70 million Pirelli shares (6.5% of the capital) in an accelerated bookbuild run by BNP Paribas at €6.50 per share, about €455 million, a 3.8% discount to Monday’s close.**

Its stake falls from 20.1% to about 13.6%, behind Marco Tronchetti Provera’s Camfin/MTP (29.9%) and Czech billionaire Michal Strnad (about 14%), who bought 14% from Sinochem earlier this year at the same price. Pirelli shares fell more than 3% in Milan. The retreat follows Rome’s use of its “golden power” screening: an April decree limited Sinochem to three of the 15 board seats, with no executive roles or strategic powers, and those restrictions apply while its stake exceeds 9.99%. Italian press links the intervention to protecting Pirelli’s Cyber Tyre technology and the company’s access to the US tyre market. A 90-day lock-up applies to the remaining stake (Websim/Intermonte).

**Why it matters for Europe.** This is the European version of the “blocking Chinese control” track the owner asked us to follow: a member state used FDI screening to push a Chinese state shareholder out of a strategic industrial company, partly so as not to lose access to the US market. Italian screening worked, but it was a national decision; the EU still has no common instrument to decide such cases, and alignment with US connected-vehicle rules is shaping European ownership choices. Note: @F_CEAFE has not posted this story.

**What to watch.** whether Sinochem drops below 10% after the lock-up, lifting the golden-power conditions; any MOFCOM or Sinochem comment.

## Commission autumn forecast: growth holds at 1.2% in the euro area, but the energy shock keeps inflation above 3%

**The European Commission’s Autumn 2026 Economic Forecast (17 November) expects EU GDP growth of 1.4% in 2026 and 2027 and 1.5% in 2028, and 1.2%, 1.2% and 1.3% in the euro area.**

Inflation is forecast at 3.3% (EU) and 3.2% (euro area) in 2026, easing to 2.3% and 2.1% only in 2028. The Commission blames the Middle East war: energy flows through Hormuz “remain severely disrupted”, Brent exceeded $100 in October, diesel prices doubled year on year and TTF gas traded above €95/MWh in late October, more than three times its late-February level, with thin buffers in strategic reserves and gas storage. Central banks, including the Fed, have started or are expected to raise rates, and the 10-year US Treasury yield was about 5.1% at the cut-off. Deficits widen to −3.6% of GDP in the EU in 2026 and −3.8% in 2028. Bloomberg summarised it as robust growth despite stubborn inflation.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The forecast puts a number on Europe’s energy dependence: imported fossil fuels are again the main driver of inflation and of the rate rises that tighten financing for defence and industrial investment. The Commission itself lists faster progress in the energy transition “to reduce Europe’s dependence on imported fossil fuels” and stronger investment in defence and industry as upside factors.

**What to watch.** Eurostat’s final October HICP; the ECB’s December meeting.
