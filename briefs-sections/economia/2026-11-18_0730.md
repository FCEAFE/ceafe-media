## Update: Nvidia stops shipping its US-licensed Hopper chips to China, two days after Washington’s remote-access rule

**Nvidia’s interim chief executive, Colette Kress, told analysts on 17 November (US time) that the company is “no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China” and that its guidance “does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included”.**

Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of those licensed chips (the H200, approved for China by President Trump last year in a deal brokered by Jensen Huang, routed through the US with a 25% levy) in the third quarter. She gave no reason. TechCrunch, citing Georgetown’s CSET, notes that Beijing had already largely refused to accept H200 deliveries, preferring to build demand for Chinese chips. The same call brought record results: revenue of $115.8 billion (+103% year on year), data-centre revenue of $107.6 billion and a Q4 forecast of $130 billion ±3%. The board chair, Harvey Jones, said Nvidia “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO”. Separately, the WSJ reported that the SEC is investigating Nvidia, and a Microsoft executive told Bloomberg that the turmoil could push customers towards rival chips.

**Linked action–reaction:** US Commerce/BIS rule on remote access to advanced AI chips (16 November) → China’s MFA calls it “economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction” and MOFCOM threatens countermeasures (17 November; 20:30 edition) → Nvidia halts its remaining licensed China sales (17 November, US time). **China’s response to the halt: none found yet** on the MOFCOM and MFA sites; Global Times (Chinese state media) framed the results against “Washington tightening restrictions on advanced chips”. Watch the MFA briefing today (Beijing afternoon).

**Why it matters for Europe.** The chip market is now set by a US rule, a US company’s board and Beijing’s purchasing policy; Europe has a say in none of them, even though the remote-access rule also covers data centres in the EU. Europe’s fastest supercomputer, JUPITER (EuroHPC/Jülich), runs on NVIDIA GH200 chips (TOP500, November 2026), so a supplier whose sales are now openly tied to US national-security oversight is a dependency, not a neutral vendor. Jefferies estimates the remote-access rule could cut $44–62 billion from Nvidia’s data-centre revenue next year (TechCrunch); Kress says the backlog means no top-line impact. What these figures reveal is an asymmetry of power, not a difference in talent or political will.

**What to watch.** MFA and MOFCOM reaction; whether the halt is formalised as a licence change by BIS; the 1 December hearing; the Trump–Xi meeting in Shenzhen.

## AMD completes its $2.9 billion purchase of Canada’s Taalas, another allied chip start-up absorbed by a US giant

**AMD (Santa Clara, US) announced on 17 November that it has completed the acquisition of Taalas (Toronto, Canada), a designer of AI inference chips built for specific models (its first chip was tailored to Meta’s Llama).**

AMD will put Taalas’ technology into its accelerator roadmap alongside its Instinct GPUs. In a filing on 4 November AMD valued the cash-and-stock deal at about $2.9 billion, of which $1.4 billion is contingent. The Globe and Mail calls it one of the largest takeovers of a Canadian tech company, after Ecolab’s $4.75 billion purchase of CoolIT this year. Taalas, founded in 2023 by former AMD engineers, had raised over $225 million and had 24 employees in February; its implied valuation in November 2025 was over $750 million. AMD also bought San Francisco’s World Labs for $8.2 billion in September and hired the engineers of Toronto’s Untether in 2025.

**Why it matters for Europe.** This is the pattern CEAFE tracks. A promising chip start-up in an allied country grows on local talent and venture money and then ends up inside a US group, so its technology and intellectual property answer to US decisions, including export controls. Europe’s start-ups face the same pull: the Atomico report published today puts US start-up funding at $450 billion this year against $81 billion in Europe. no new purchase of a European strategic firm by a US buyer was reported since the previous edition. The opposite flow did happen: Sweden’s EQT agreed to buy Flatiron Energy, a US battery-storage developer with a 24 GWh portfolio (closing expected Q4 2026).

**What to watch.** AMD’s integration plans; Canadian investment-review debate; any US bid for European chip or AI start-ups (Helsing, Neura Robotics and Quantum Systems have each raised more than $1 billion recently, per Atomico).

## BASF readies a new bid for Evonik, offering job and site guarantees to win over Berlin and Düsseldorf

**BASF is preparing another offer for its German peer Evonik, after Evonik rejected a sweetened bid worth more than €11 billion (€24 a share), Bloomberg and Reuters reported on 17 November.**

In a draft letter to the North Rhine-Westphalia government, members of the Bundestag and the RAG Foundation, Evonik’s largest shareholder with about 43%, BASF offers to rule out layoffs for a period still to be set, keep all German production sites and invest in R&D and greener production. The amounts and years are left as placeholders. “In a combined company, existing commitments to employees and sites will be honored and expanded,” the letter says. A new offer could come within days. NRW’s government repeated that Evonik’s independence should be protected, and Evonik’s top labour representative said BASF’s pledges offered “no additional value” if they merely restate existing protections. In October BASF’s CEO Markus Kamieth promised to honour Evonik’s employment guarantees until 2032 and to keep running the Marl and Wesseling chemical parks (about 3,500 jobs), which Evonik plans to sell.

**Why it matters for Europe.** A European consolidation in a strategic industry, chemicals, that is under pressure from energy prices far above those in the US and from Chinese overcapacity. A larger European champion is in line with the scale argument CEAFE makes. The test is whether it brings investment rather than closures, which is what the regional government and the unions are now negotiating. It follows Berlin’s plans to overhaul takeover law after UniCredit–Commerzbank (20:30 edition).

**What to watch.** The new offer price; RAG Foundation board decision; whether Berlin’s new takeover rules apply.

## Washington sells its LNG in Warsaw and Manila, as US officials boast that Europe pays 11 times the American gas price

**US Energy Secretary Chris Wright arrived in Warsaw on 17 November (“Great to be back in Warsaw for P-TEC”) for the seventh Partnership for Transatlantic Energy Cooperation, co-organised by the US Department of Energy, the Atlantic Council and Poland’s Ministry of Energy: a business forum today (18 November) and a ministerial meeting tomorrow, with sessions on long-term LNG supply security, transatlantic LNG and civil nuclear cooperation.**

EU Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen and the deputy prime ministers of Czechia, Slovakia and Hungary are among the invited guests. Wright wrote that “together with our European allies” Washington is working to “unleash energy abundance”. And now, with our new main supplier, gas prices in Europe are 11 times higher than in the US.” He illustrated it with an October chart by US Interior Secretary Doug Burgum comparing Henry Hub with Europe’s TTF and Asia’s JKM. The same evening, in Manila, President Trump said that “thanks to a historic investment from South Korea, we also just approved a colossal liquefied natural gas pipeline in Alaska”. Yonhap notes the White House has tied $54 billion to the project, while Seoul says its participation is still subject to a commercial-viability review, and that Trump had threatened to “charge them more” if Korea did not invest.

**Why it matters for Europe.** US LNG has replaced Russian pipeline gas as Europe’s main supplier. US officials present the price gap as a selling point for American energy, while for European industry it is a competitive handicap. Washington is pushing the same deal on Asian allies, linking investment pledges to trade terms. Energy dependence is no longer on Moscow but on Washington’s export and pricing policy.

**What to watch.** P-TEC ministerial on 19 November (any LNG or nuclear deals, the Commission’s position); the EU gas-storage trajectory (IEA, 20:30 edition: lowest since 2013); Hormuz traffic; Korean decision on Alaska LNG.

## European start-ups head for $81 billion this year, less than a fifth of the $450 billion going to US start-ups

**Atomico’s annual State of European Tech report, published on 18 November, expects European technology start-ups to raise $81 billion in 2026.**

That is 62% more than last year and the second-highest total since the survey began in 2015, but far below the $450 billion expected for US start-ups. German start-ups are set to raise $13.4 billion (+89%), mostly going to established companies. Military AI developer Helsing, robot maker Neura Robotics and drone maker Quantum Systems have each raised more than $1 billion in recent months. “All the ingredients are actually there: talent, capital and demand. But they are either blocked or misallocated,” Atomico partner Tom Wehmeier told Reuters. Europeans hold $50 trillion in savings, but “only 0.01 per cent of that flows into European venture capital”.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The gap explains why European start-ups end up selling to US buyers or moving to the US. Europe’s savings exist, but they are spread across 27 national markets with no single capital market to channel them into European technology. Europe has resources; what it lacks, time and again, is the scale that only joint action can provide.

**What to watch.** The full Atomico report (country breakdown, defence-tech share); the Commission’s savings and investments union.
