Update: Beijing declines to add to its Megaspeed position; Singapore keeps investigating

At the Chinese MFA’s 24 November briefing, asked by AFP about the US blacklisting of Singapore-based Megaspeed International (topic 1 of the 08:30 edition), spokesperson Guo Jiakun said: “We have made clear China’s position on the relevant issue more than once. I have nothing further to add.” On Jensen Huang’s reinstatement at Nvidia: “We have no comment on this.” Singapore’s Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said Singapore will keep investigating Megaspeed (Reuters). The Global Times (Chinese state media) argues in a “GT Voice” editorial that Huang’s return “is unlikely to silence his opponents” as Washington has tightened chip restrictions over the past two weeks; Bloomberg reports Beijing warning that the comeback does not end the risk of further US chip controls.

Why it matters for Europe: Beijing’s low-key answer contrasts with the speed with which Malaysia and Singapore aligned with Washington’s listing: the extraterritorial measure is being enforced by third states while the target keeps its powder dry for the trade talks.

China warns Mexico over tariffs on Chinese goods and “third-party interference”

China’s Commerce Minister Wang Wentao urged Mexico to take seriously China’s “grave concerns” over tariffs on Chinese goods, to use trade remedies prudently, to address problems faced by Chinese investors and to safeguard bilateral trade “from third-party interference” — a reference to US pressure on Mexico (Global Times; SCMP). Mexico’s Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard said Mexico was open to an institutionalised tariff dialogue with China (Global Times).

Why it matters for Europe: Mexico is being squeezed between Washington’s demand to raise barriers against China (as the USMCA review approaches) and Beijing’s counter-pressure — the same choice the EU faces each time it is asked to align its trade defences with US priorities.

Equinor and Germany’s VNG sign an 18-year gas contract, the longest Norwegian deal since 1986

Norway’s Equinor will supply up to 15.3 TWh (about 1.4 bcm) of natural gas a year to Leipzig-based VNG from 1 January 2027 to the end of 2044, delivered at Trading Hub Europe; pricing reflects market terms, other details are confidential (Equinor). It is the longest Norwegian gas sales agreement since the Troll agreements of 1986. The contract was signed in Oslo on 24 November by Equinor CEO Anders Opedal and VNG CEO Ulf Heitmüller. Germany is Equinor’s largest gas market.

Why it matters for Europe: a long-term European pipeline contract, between a Norwegian and a German company, is a rare counterweight to the growing dependence on US LNG (US supply: 58% of EU LNG imports in 2025, per ACER, as reported on 18 November). Intra-European energy deals of this kind reduce exposure both to Russia and to Washington’s “energy dominance” leverage.

Key facts: volume up to 15.3 TWh/yr (~1.4 bcm/yr); term 1 Jan 2027–end-2044; first Norwegian gas to eastern Germany in 1996 (Equinor).

European gas rises with the cold snap and storage at 68.9% while oil falls to three-month lows

The Dutch TTF front-month gas contract rose about 1.25% to €90.89/MWh in early trading on 24 November as forecasts pointed to a prolonged cold spell (Investing.com/Reuters). EU gas storage was only 68.9% full on 23 November, against a five-year average of 89% and about 82% a year earlier (Gas Infrastructure Europe via OilPrice.com). Oil moved the other way: Brent touched its lowest since 27 August and traded around $88.6–89.1 a barrel, WTI around $79.5–80, as Libya’s Sharara pipeline reopened and analysts pointed to a supply surplus despite the Hormuz closure and new mine incidents (CNBC/Reuters; The Independent). Japan is poised to buy more spot LNG as gas-fired power becomes profitable again, and Chinese refiners have stepped up crude purchases to rebuild stocks (Bloomberg).

Why it matters for Europe: Europe pays a premium for gas in winter just as Asian buyers return to the spot LNG market; with Henry Hub around $3.2/MMBtu (ENGIE EnergyScan), the price gap with the US that weighs on European industry persists.

Spain’s People’s Party promises a tougher trade line on China if it wins the 29 November election

Spain’s conservative People’s Party (PP), leading the polls before the 29 November general election, would back a more hawkish trade approach to China if its leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo becomes prime minister, its economy spokesman Alberto Nadal told Bloomberg: Spain’s stance would align with those who see that “China is different” and that “Europe needs a clear China policy”. Nadal said future Chinese investment should involve Spanish partners and not be mere assembly plants to obtain a “Made in Europe” label; a PP government could review contracts with Chinese suppliers such as Huawei (Bloomberg via Placera/Headliner). Spain’s trade deficit with China was $41.1 bn in 2025; a Fedea study counts about €14.5 bn of completed and announced Chinese projects since 2019 (Bloomberg, as reported by Headliner).

Why it matters for Europe: the Sánchez government has been one of the EU’s most China-friendly; a shift in Madrid would change the balance inside the EU on trade defence. Requiring local partners and real value added is a sound industrial demand; excluding suppliers such as Huawei, as Washington asks, closes European options without a European alternative in place.

Demand for Fujitsu’s Monaka server chips beats expectations as buyers seek alternatives to US and Chinese suppliers

Initial demand for Fujitsu’s new 2-nanometre, Arm-based Monaka server CPUs is exceeding expectations, driven by clients in Japan, the US and Europe seeking alternatives to US and Chinese suppliers for defence, finance, manufacturing and the public sector, CTO Vivek Mahajan told Bloomberg. Most of the 45 companies that received samples in August are likely to order; Fujitsu is in talks with TSMC for more capacity. It expects ¥35 bn (about $220 m) of Monaka sales next fiscal year and ¥500 bn a year by 2035. “We are… probably [the] only ones outside the U.S. that make such a cutting-edge CPU,” Mahajan said (Japan Times/Bloomberg).

Why it matters for Europe: European buyers are turning to a Japanese CPU (made in Taiwan) for “sovereign” infrastructure because Europe has no cutting-edge server processor of its own in volume production — a measure of the gap the EU Chips Act has yet to close.

Germany’s ifo index rises for a seventh month; an ECB hawk talks of rate hikes

The ifo Business Climate Index rose to 90.6 in November from 90.1 in October, the seventh consecutive increase and the highest since May 2023 (Trading Economics); current conditions 89.4, expectations 91.9. Ifo president Clemens Fuest said the recovery “lacks breadth”: manufacturing sentiment fell after six increases and expectations there slipped back into negative territory (ifo). ECB Governing Council member Gediminas Simkus said it is “prudent” to raise rates to levels that start to constrain the economy as price pressures broaden beyond oil (Bloomberg).

Why it matters for Europe: the German upswing is driven by services and public spending, not by industry — consistent with the deindustrialisation trend.
