Update: US chip controls reach Southeast Asia — Alibaba calls the blacklisting of its Singapore unit “erroneous”, Singapore pledges to work with Washington

The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security added seven Southeast Asian entities to its Entity List on Monday, among them Alibaba’s Singapore subsidiary Apex Enterprise Solutions and Megaspeed International, a cloud company under US investigation for suspected circumvention of chip export controls, citing a “significant risk” of diverting advanced computing products; the other listed firms are in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand (Global Times, Chinese state media, citing Reuters and Bloomberg). Alibaba responded: “Adding Apex Enterprise Solutions to the Entity List is erroneous because Apex has never diverted advanced computing chips subject to US export restrictions… We will seek all legal avenues to protect our rights and reputation” (statement to The Straits Times; Reuters). According to a person cited by Reuters, Apex paid around $4.2 billion to Megaspeed and other firms as prepayment to rent computing capacity in Southeast Asia and took some of their assets as a pledge. Singapore’s Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said his country is “working with the US” to stop companies “exploiting” it to transship and divert chips, and is running its own investigation (SCMP, owned by Alibaba).

The listing requires a licence for all items subject to US export rules, with a presumption of denial; the rule is due for publication on 25 November. The Global Times frames it as Washington “increasingly” extending chip controls to overseas subsidiaries, while Mike Kuiken, vice-chair of the US–China Economic and Security Review Commission, told Bloomberg that China is doing “everything they can to smuggle” or “steal” advanced US chips. At its 24 November briefing, China’s foreign ministry had nothing further to add on Megaspeed (14:30 edition). Chinese state media are already asking whether the Netherlands “may face a choice” as its foreign minister visits China (Global Times opinion).

Why it matters for Europe: US export controls now reach a Chinese group’s subsidiary in a third country for renting computing capacity there, and third states such as Singapore help enforce them. The same extraterritorial logic applies to European firms and data centres — ASML is the prime example — and Europe has no reciprocal tool and no plan to answer it.

What to watch: Alibaba’s legal challenge; a formal reaction from China’s Ministry of Commerce; any Dutch or EU statement during Foreign Minister Berendsen’s China visit.

China’s BYD in exclusive talks to buy INEOS’s French car plant as INEOS moves production to the US

BYD, China’s largest carmaker, said on 24 November that it has entered exclusive negotiations to buy the Hambach plant in Moselle, France, from INEOS Automotive, subject to consultation with works councils; the amount was not disclosed. According to Reuters, it would be the first purchase of a European car plant by a Chinese automaker: rivals have preferred to rent space (Dongfeng and Leapmotor at Stellantis plants in France and Spain; Geely at a Ford plant in Spain). French President Emmanuel Macron welcomed the project as “a direct consequence” of his economic policy.

On the same day INEOS Automotive (UK, owned by Jim Ratcliffe) announced that it will begin producing its Grenadier and Quartermaster in the United States in 2028, as the US accounts for 70% of Grenadier sales, with a final site decision “within the next few months”; Bloomberg says the shift aims to avoid US tariffs. Production would continue at Hambach in the medium term. Hambach is the former Smart/Mercedes-Benz site bought by INEOS in 2020. BYD wants European production ahead of the EU’s planned “Made in Europe” local-content rules and will start mass production in Hungary in early 2027; an EU cap on imports of Chinese hybrid vehicles applies from 1 December 2026 (France 3 Grand Est).

Why it matters for Europe: one story shows both sides of the pressure on European industry. US tariffs push a European-based producer to move production to the US, with jobs and supply chain following, while Chinese capital fills the gap to secure access to the EU market under the coming local-content rules. Europe keeps the plant, but depends on others’ decisions: the owner relocates to the US, and the buyer is Chinese.

What to watch: works-council consultation at Hambach; INEOS’s choice of US site; the final shape of the EU’s “Made in Europe” rules.

Japan pays its 2027 ICC contribution early to shield the court from US sanctions

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told International Criminal Court President Tomoko Akane on 24 November that Japan will pay its full 2027 contribution to the court by the end of 2026, after the ICC sought early payment from its largest contributor (CNA, Reuters). Japan supplies about 15% of the ICC budget, followed by Germany, France, the UK, Italy, South Korea and Canada; the early payment gives the court funds before US sanctions take full effect in April 2027 (CNA).

In the CEAFE archive: in December 2025 the ICC rejected new US sanctions designations against two of its judges, and in August 2026, when Takaichi called US sanctions on the Japanese ICC chief “very unfortunate”, @F_CEAFE commented that Japan was doing the same as the Europeans — “just words”. Tuesday’s payment is the first concrete step.

Why it matters for Europe: a close US ally acts to blunt US sanctions on an international court, while no similar step was reported in this window from the EU member states that follow Japan in the ranking (Germany, France, Italy). The EU has its own instrument against extraterritorial sanctions, the Blocking Statute; the question is whether Europeans will defend the court as a bloc.

What to watch: whether Germany, France, Italy or the EU follow with early payments or use of the Blocking Statute before April 2027.

The Commission’s Autumn Package flags the EU’s growing financial exposure to the United States

The European Commission adopted the 2027 European Semester Autumn Package on 24 November in Strasbourg. Its new European Macroeconomic Report highlights “the EU’s growing financial exposure to the United States, underlining the need for deeper integration of European capital markets”, and says the EU is narrowing the gap with the US in AI adoption.

The euro-area recommendation calls for deepening the Single Market, advancing the Savings and Investments Union and making room for higher investment in defence, energy and innovation; the Commission projects a marked rise in the aggregate government deficit in 2026 and a rising debt-to-GDP ratio.

Why it matters for Europe: European savings that finance the US economy instead of European firms are another form of dependence; the Commission itself now names it.

What to watch: progress on the Savings and Investments Union and how EU member states make room for defence investment under the fiscal rules.
