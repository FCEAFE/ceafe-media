## US House China Committee: no FAA certification for Chinese aircraft, curbs on investment in Chinese aerospace, and “heightened scrutiny” of French takeovers of US aerospace firms

**The House Select Committee on the CCP (chair John Moolenaar) published on 19 November a year-long investigation, “China’s wingmen: How Western government policy enabled China’s rise in the skies”.**

It says American and European aerospace contractors transferred technology and know-how to Chinese aerospace firms through joint ventures and licences that ended up helping China’s military air power. It recommends that the Treasury restrict outbound investment in China’s aerospace sector (and that Congress write this into the COINS Act), that the FAA refuse to certify Chinese-built aircraft, that federal contracts be pulled from contractors (Reuters names Airbus) that keep investing in China’s aerospace base, and that acquisitions of US aerospace companies by French firms face “heightened scrutiny and a presumption of denial” until they unwind ties with China’s civil-military ecosystem. @F_CEAFE posted the story at 08:10.

**Why it matters for Europe.** This is an explicit proposal to use US contracts, investment screening (CFIUS) and certification as levers over a European company and a European state. Airbus, Europe’s main aerospace champion, competes with Boeing in China (COMAC is the future third competitor), and the report singles out France: French aerospace firms are said to have “much stronger ties to the Chinese aerospace industrial base than American companies”, and to market themselves as “beyond the reach of US restrictions” (e.g. Airbus selling satellite imagery without US restrictions). If adopted, French buyers of US aerospace assets would face a presumption of denial, while US buyers of European firms face no such test in Europe. It fits the US double standard and Europe’s lack of reciprocity described in the editorial line. Once again, a single European state (France) is singled out alone, and only a common EU response on investment screening would carry comparable weight.

**What to watch.** Airbus and French government reaction; whether the Treasury extends the Outbound Investment Security Program to aerospace; China’s MOFCOM/MFA response (none found in the 20 November MFA briefing).

## Update: US chip curbs bite in China: Alibaba may cut its 20 GW computing goal, Chinese AI start-ups slump, TSMC suspends some Chinese orders

**Reuters (20 November) reports that the series of US measures this week, including the BIS remote-access rule, Nvidia’s halt of some shipments to China and a US proposal requiring American companies to disclose use of Chinese AI models, “further pummelled” Chinese tech stocks.**

Alibaba CEO Eddie Wu said the rule will affect its products next year, could force it to lower its long-term target of 20 gigawatts of computing capacity, and that cloud revenue growth may slow to 30–45% next year from 53%. Tencent President Martin Lau said the curbs will slow its next-generation model and accelerate the shift to domestic chips. Z.AI has fallen 22% and MiniMax 34% in two weeks; Iluvatar CoreX (ByteDance supplier) fell 17%. TSMC suspended some previously permitted advanced orders from Chinese customers after the new licensing requirement on overseas data centres.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The rule covers data centres in third countries, including the EU, so European cloud operators serving Chinese clients are now inside a US licensing regime. The fact that a US rule decides what a Taiwanese foundry and a European data centre may do is the core of the extraterritorial-controls thread. China’s answer is to speed up self-sufficiency, which over time shrinks the market for European chip-tool makers such as ASML. Europe pays the cost of a contest between two powers in which it has neither a seat nor a vote.

**What to watch.** MOFCOM response; whether EU data-centre operators seek licences.

## Export controls, the other way: China’s licence requirement for drone engines and parts is now in force; Kyiv welcomes it, Russia’s Shahed supply chain is the target

**Since 18 November Chinese exporters need a MOFCOM dual-use licence for a list of drone items: autopilots, drones with 30+ minutes’ endurance, engines above 16 kW, turbojets with thrust of 400 N or more, high-power radio modules, heavy brushless motors (≥48 V, ≥3 kW), speed controllers, large carbon-fibre propeller blades and related software and technology (MOFCOM, MAC and CMC Equipment Development Department Announcement No.**

49 of 2026, issued 19 October). Some of the engines covered are used in the Shahed/Geran drones Russia assembles at Alabuga. Ukraine’s sanctions commissioner Vladyslav Vlasiuk thanked China but said anti-jamming antennas, navigation modules, gimbal cameras and servos remain outside the controls (“only part of what matters… but it is a start”). @F_CEAFE reposted the Kyiv Independent report at 08:34.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The measure took effect the day Putin arrived in Shenzhen for the trilateral meeting with Trump and Xi; Beijing used its own export-control law, not a Western sanctions regime. Europe, which sanctions Chinese firms for supplying Russia, now depends on a decision in Beijing for the volume of drones hitting Ukraine’s (and Europe’s largest) energy infrastructure. MOFCOM frames it as non-proliferation and “dynamic adjustment”, and says compliant licence applications will be approved. A reminder that the levers over the war’s supply chains are in Washington and Beijing, not in Brussels.

**What to watch.** Licence approval data; whether the EU adjusts its listings of Chinese suppliers.

## The AI sell-off spreads: global stocks post their longest losing run since September 2023

**The MSCI All Country World Index fell 0.7% on 20 November, its seventh straight drop and the longest run since September 2023, as the AI trade unwinds (Bloomberg).**

Anthropic fell nearly 6% in early trading, partly reversing its 17% first-day gain. The Stoxx Europe 600 fell 1.1% by late morning (more than 430 members down, technology leading), a fifth daily fall; MSCI Asia Pacific -3.3%, heading for its worst week since the start of the pandemic; Australian equities had their worst week in more than four years. Investors bought bonds: the German 10-year yield fell 8 bp to 3.33%, the US 10-year 6 bp to 4.99%. The dollar is near a 19-month high, set for a fourth weekly gain; the yen led G10 currencies.

**Why it matters for Europe.** The AI sell-off is being imported into Europe through tech stocks, while the financing side shows the asymmetry: SpaceX sold $32 billion of debt in a day to buy AI chips (part at yields above 8%), whereas Europe’s annual bond sales, above €2 trillion for the first time this year (2026), are driven mainly by government borrowing. A strong dollar and a near-certain Fed hike in December (two more priced for 2027) tighten conditions for European importers of US energy and arms paid in dollars.

**What to watch.** US close tonight; Fed 9 December; ECB December meeting.

## Update: Commerzbank raises its profit outlook as UniCredit’s takeover nears

**Commerzbank CEO Bettina Orlopp said on 20 November the bank now “clearly” expects €3.5 billion of net profit in 2026, above the previous “at least €3.4 billion”.**

In her first remarks since this week’s agreement between Berlin and UniCredit, she welcomed the clarity on the Frankfurt headquarters and the stock-market listing; UniCredit CEO Andrea Orcel had given Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil eight binding commitments (listing kept in Frankfurt, key decisions taken in Germany, expansion of the Mittelstand business) (Reuters; Reuters German service via onvista).

**Why it matters for Europe.** An Italian–German bank merger is the kind of intra-European consolidation the editorial line sees as positive: scale in banking union rather than US banks or funds taking European lenders. The open question is UniCredit’s plan to cut Commerzbank’s international network, which serves German exporters.

## Energy dependence: Russian LNG still reaches the EU while Türkiye signs a new Novatek supply contract

**Bloomberg (20 November) notes that, despite the EU’s drive to wean itself off Russian energy since 2022, Russian LNG is still entering the bloc ahead of a full ban.**

Russia’s Novatek said on 18 November it had signed an LNG supply contract with Türkiye and is discussing larger volumes and gas investments; buyer, volume, price and start date were not disclosed (TASS, RIA, Anadolu — Russian state media, attributed). @F_CEAFE reposted Turkish analyst Gizem Özpinar’s comparison: non-EU Türkiye signs with Novatek for its own energy security while EU member Greece is criticised for its choices (her framing).

**Why it matters for Europe.** Europe is replacing Russian gas with US LNG at prices well above US levels (the 11x gap covered on 18 November), while neighbours such as Türkiye keep buying Russian supply and can resell energy and industrial goods into the EU. Iran is also readying a tanker convoy at Kharg Island to test the US oil blockade (Euronews); any incident there would hit Brent, still near $90. Dependence has changed supplier, not nature.

**What to watch.** EU timetable for the full Russian LNG ban; Kharg convoy.
