Update: Beijing’s commerce ministry threatens countermeasures if Washington keeps its global chip rule

At 13:20 Madrid (20:20 Beijing) China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) published its own answer to the US remote-access rule that the 13:30 edition led with.

Until then only the Foreign Ministry had spoken. The spokesperson said the US “has generalized the concept of national security, abused export controls, and arbitrarily interfered in the normal trade and cooperation between Chinese enterprises and those of other countries”. The ministry added that the rule departs from the “important consensus reached by the two heads of state”, undermines “market rules and the international economic and trade order” and disrupts the stability of global semiconductor supply chains. It asked Washington to “return to the right track of resolving differences through consultation and cooperation”. Then came the warning: “Should the US side insist on going its own way, China will take countermeasures to firmly safeguard its national sovereignty, security and development interests.” Bloomberg reported it as China warning the US that it would retaliate. @F_CEAFE reposted Global Times’ account of the statement (Chinese state media), as well as its earlier coverage of the rule.

Linked action–reaction: the US Commerce rule of 16 November (BIS, written “by and at the direction of” Treasury and the NSC) → MFA, 17 November: “typical economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction” (13:30 edition) → MOFCOM, 17 November: a conditional threat of countermeasures. MOFCOM, not the MFA, is the body that runs China’s Unreliable Entity List and its export-control lists, including rare earths, so its words carry operational weight.

Why it matters for Europe. the rule applies to data centres anywhere in the world, the EU included, and Europe is caught between both sides. If Beijing retaliates through rare earths or its own export controls, European industry will pay as a bystander for a measure that Brussels neither negotiated nor approved. TNW notes that the dispute comes days before Xi Jinping hosts Donald Trump at the APEC summit in Shenzhen, and that the tariff truce from last year ends in January, according to Bloomberg. The EU has no seat at that table, even though its data-centre operators and its supply chains are on it.

What to watch. whether MOFCOM puts US firms on the Unreliable Entity List or tightens rare-earth licensing before the Shenzhen summit; whether the Commission says anything at all about a US rule that covers EU data centres.

Update: Nvidia sets up a board committee on export controls, and Jensen Huang will face three House committees on 1 December

On 17 November NVIDIA’s board created a standing National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee to oversee national-security policies, export-control compliance and global trade-risk management.

Suzanne Nora Johnson chairs it, and Stephen C. Neal and John O. Dabiri are members. The committee will meet at least quarterly and may hire independent legal, national-security and trade-compliance experts. The WSJ and Reuters described it as the company’s first big move since Jensen Huang resigned as CEO over his relationship with a woman US officials suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. The same day, the House Select Committee on the CCP announced that Huang and NVIDIA general counsel Tim Teter had “voluntarily agreed” to appear before it, the House Foreign Affairs Committee and the House Intelligence Committee. These are closed-door, transcribed interviews, not public hearings: Huang on 1 December and Teter on 2 December. The chairmen (Moolenaar, Mast, Crawford) say Americans “need answers on Mr. Huang and NVIDIA’s role in allowing dangerous PRC actors to access our most advanced chips”. @F_CEAFE reposted both the NVIDIA release and the committee’s statement. On markets, Bloomberg reported that emerging-market stocks fell to a seven-month low as the tech selloff extended ahead of Nvidia’s earnings, and Reuters wrote that the “Nvidia turmoil marks the end of easy money for the AI trade”.

Why it matters for Europe. the world’s key AI-chip supplier now has a board body that answers, in practice, to Washington’s national-security agenda, and Congress is turning chip sales into an intelligence matter. European cloud and AI projects depend on that supplier. Every tightening of its compliance will reach European customers too (see the remote-access rule, topic 1), and no European chip can replace it in this class.

What to watch. Nvidia’s quarterly results this week; what the 1 December interview reveals about chip diversion through Southeast Asia (Megaspeed case, 13:30 edition).

Berlin rewrites its takeover law after UniCredit’s creeping approach on Commerzbank

According to a finance ministry draft seen by Reuters and Börsen-Zeitung, Germany will tighten its takeover rules to stop “low-ball” bids and “creeping in”.

Today a bidder must make a mandatory offer when it reaches 30% of the voting rights. The draft adds further mandatory-offer thresholds at 35%, 40%, 45% and 50%, so a bidder can no longer start with a minimum-price offer and then build its stake step by step. It also brings in a “put up or shut up” rule: if takeover speculation emerges, the target can demand an answer within four weeks, and a bidder that declines or stays silent cannot announce an offer for six months. Bidders would also have to disclose short derivatives and the instruments held by their counterparties, such as investment banks, even below the normal reporting thresholds. Börsen-Zeitung says UniCredit built part of its Commerzbank stake that way without the market knowing. The reform is meant to take effect in the first quarter of 2027 and, according to the ministry, will not apply to the UniCredit–Commerzbank deal, which Brussels cleared this week (2030 edition of 16 November). The federal government still holds 12.5% of Commerzbank.

Why it matters for Europe. this is an intra-European deal (Italy’s UniCredit buying Germany’s Commerzbank), the kind of cross-border consolidation that Banking Union was supposed to allow. Germany’s answer is to raise national barriers. The tools themselves (more transparency, protecting minority shareholders) are reasonable and line up with EU law. But the trigger is telling: a large member state is building new defences against a European bidder, not a US or Chinese one. Europe keeps a fragmented banking market of national champions, while US banks operate at continental scale.

What to watch. reactions from Rome and the Commission; whether other member states copy the “put up or shut up” rule.

Vestas asks Brussels to keep Chinese software out of Europe’s energy grids, and invokes reciprocity with Beijing

Henrik Andersen, CEO of the Danish wind-turbine maker Vestas, told Reuters that the EU should say a “very upfront ‘no thank you’” to Chinese components and technology with a software element in critical energy infrastructure.

“The energy assets you build now last 30 to 50 years, and you have to make sure you can control them yourself,” he said. He proposed that about half of EU wind assets be made in Europe, and that control systems and software be treated separately from that requirement. Andersen recalled that China already applies this logic: Chinese authorities told Vestas it could no longer monitor the sensors of its own turbines in China or update their software remotely from Europe. “China put a marker in the sand five years ago, and now it’s time that we say that marker counts on both sides.” He rejected a blanket ban on Chinese parts, but asked for equal market access: “Eleven a side and a referee in the middle.” China’s Ming Yang replied that it stores all turbine data in Europe and allows no remote access without the operator’s approval. MOFCOM has threatened “strong countermeasures” if the EU adopts its revised Cybersecurity Act.

Why it matters for Europe. this is a clear case of the reciprocity CEAFE calls for. China shut a European firm out of its own turbines, while Europe is still debating whether it may do the same. The key distinction is to protect control of critical infrastructure on Europe’s own terms, without importing US blacklists. As with chips (topic 1), whoever holds the software holds the switch. (Andersen’s company would also gain if Chinese rivals are kept out, as Reuters notes.)

What to watch. the trilogue on the revised Cybersecurity Act; any MOFCOM response.

Spain’s Hernández de Cos moves closer to the ECB presidency in a Franco-German package

Germany’s finance ministry put forward Princeton economist Markus Brunnermeier as the European Central Bank’s next chief economist, replacing Philip Lane, whose term ends in May.

The cabinet is due to approve the nomination on Wednesday 18 November, the deadline for submissions to the Eurogroup. According to Reuters (via RTÉ) and the Irish Times, the move seals a package agreed between Merz and Macron. Spain’s Pablo Hernández de Cos would succeed Christine Lagarde, who leaves early on 7 February, and France would take the markets-operations seat left by Isabel Schnabel in January. Laurence Boone is the likely French candidate, according to the Irish Times. The Dutch candidate Klaas Knot is expected to withdraw. An EU official quoted by the Irish Times called the three-way package a “done deal” now that Spain and Italy back de Cos. EU leaders are expected to endorse it at a summit next week, before the European Parliament is consulted.

Why it matters for Europe. for the first time a Spaniard would lead the euro’s central bank, and a German would hold the chief economist’s post for the first time since 2011. The euro is the EU’s most federal instrument and its main tool of monetary sovereignty against the dollar. Even so, the board is shared out by national quota (France, Germany and Italy in practice hold permanent seats), and leaders are hurrying to finish before the 2027 French election and possible stress on French bonds.

What to watch. German cabinet decision (18 November); the EU summit next week.

WTO: trade restrictions in force hit a record $5.4 trillion, as the US signs deal after deal on its own terms

The WTO Trade Monitoring Report, presented on 17 November and reposted by @F_CEAFE, finds that members introduced 333 new restrictive or non-facilitating measures in the first half of 2026, more than the 300 recorded in all of 2025.

They cover $2.0 trillion (8.0%) of world imports and $794 billion (3.2%) of exports. The stock of such measures introduced since 2009 and still in force reached a record: $5.4 trillion, or 21.3% of world imports, and $1.5 trillion (6.3%) of world exports, as of 30 June 2026. The WTO also notes that trade-facilitating measures increased. The same day in Manila, Donald Trump and Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul signed a joint statement on the “substantial conclusion” of a US–Thailand Agreement on Reciprocal Trade. Trump says Thailand will eliminate all tariffs on US exports, remove non-tariff barriers on US vehicles, medical devices, pharmaceuticals, ethanol and poultry, and buy about $2.6 billion of extra US farm goods. The White House statement lists no US concessions. According to the Bangkok Post, the 2025 framework provides for a 19% baseline US tariff on Thai goods, and since July Thailand has also faced an extra 12.5% tariff from a forced-labour investigation. @F_CEAFE reposted the White House clip.

Why it matters for Europe. the figures confirm the drift from multilateral rules to bilateral deals in which Washington sets the terms one partner at a time: tariffs scrapped on one side, a baseline tariff kept on the other, plus purchase commitments. The EU, the largest trading bloc and the one most attached to the WTO, is losing the system that protected it. It negotiates with Washington under the same asymmetry, despite being much larger than Thailand.

What to watch. signature of the US–Thailand agreement “in the coming weeks”; other ASEAN deals around the Manila summit.
