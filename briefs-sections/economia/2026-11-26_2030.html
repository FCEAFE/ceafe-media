Altria confirms it is considering an all-cash offer for Britain’s Imperial Brands; Japan Tobacco is also studying a deal

US group Altria (Richmond, Virginia; Marlboro in the US) confirmed on 26 November that it is “considering a possible offer” for London-listed Imperial Brands and that any offer “is likely to be solely in cash”. It also said it is “in discussions with a third party regarding its possible acquisition of part of the business of Imperial Brands”, with no agreement yet (Altria, Rule 2.4 statement). Imperial’s board said it “has not had any discussions with, or received any approach or proposal from, Altria or the third party” and told shareholders to take no action. Under the UK Takeover Code Altria must make a firm offer or walk away by 17:00 London time on 24 December 2026 (Imperial Brands RNS). Bloomberg reported that Japan Tobacco is also studying a deal, possibly with a partner, splitting Imperial’s assets by region. Imperial shares jumped as much as 17.6% to 3,562p, valuing it at £26.77bn (about US$35bn) (Reuters via Investing.com).

Why it matters for Europe: it would be one of the largest takeovers of a British company in a decade. London-listed firms are cheap: Imperial trades at 8.3 times forward earnings against 12.6 for Altria and 17.7 for JT (LSEG via Reuters). Tobacco is not a strategic sector under CEAFE’s US-takeover list, so it is not added to that list. But it shows the same pattern: depressed European valuations make European headquarters and brands (Davidoff, Gauloises) easy prey for US and Asian capital. Imperial announced up to 4,600 job cuts last week.

Key facts

  • Buyer: Altria Group (US), market value about US$126.9bn; possible partner: an unnamed third party for part of the business. Rival interest: Japan Tobacco (Japan), about US$69.5bn.
  • Target: Imperial Brands plc (UK), market value £26.77bn after the jump.
  • Deadline: “put up or shut up” by 24 December 2026.
  • Altria and JT already run a smoke-free joint venture (2022). RBC says a joint bid “would neatly swerve anti-trust objections”.

Update: Section 301 “overcapacity” tariffs: Singapore is “very disappointed”, partners hit back, and Global Times says Brussels “waved the white flag”

The US Trade Representative’s Section 301 findings on “structural excess capacity” in 14 economies, including the EU, published on 25 November (US time), keep drawing replies. Singapore’s Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry said: “Singapore disagrees with the USTR’s findings and we are very disappointed with the proposed actions.” The USTR proposes up to 20% on Singapore’s exports, cut to 10% with a reciprocal trade agreement. METI stressed that the US has run a trade surplus with Singapore for over 20 years, US$33bn in 2025 (METI). Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong said the effective levy on Singapore’s exports would rise from 7.8% to 10.3% (Reuters). AP reports that China and other partners are pushing back and that the duties are likely to take effect early next year. They are intended to rebuild the tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February. European stocks nonetheless rallied as “tariff fears ease” (Reuters).

The reaction to Europe’s reply is the new element. On 25 November Commission spokesman Olof Gill said the EU “strongly reject[s] the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity” and that “Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity… much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action” (RTÉ). A Global Times [Chinese state media] editorial answered: “The moment Washington raised its tariff stick, the European Commission immediately waved the white flag, willingly offering itself as an accomplice in exchange for being spared.” It said that “what Europe needs is strategic autonomy, not strategic subservience”.

Why it matters for Europe: the EU’s 15% ceiling from the 2025 Turnberry deal is again on the table, and the Commission’s first instinct was to offer Washington a joint front against China rather than defend the rules. Beijing is exploiting the contradiction. Europe is caught between US tariffs and Chinese pressure, and has no reciprocity tool of its own in use.

Key facts

  • 14 economies targeted; Singapore up to 20% (10% with a deal); EU expects the 15% cap to be respected “in full, including when combined with existing duties” (Gill).
  • US trade surplus with Singapore: US$27bn (2024), US$33bn (2025) (METI).
  • Public consultation period still to come; measures likely early 2027 (AP).

The EU Quantum Act shuts Chinese firms out of quantum procurement but leaves the door open to US suppliers; Beijing calls it discrimination

The draft Quantum Act unveiled by the Commission on 25 November says that “given the possible impact of quantum technologies for security and the Union’s strategic interests and technological autonomy, it is necessary to restrict procurement procedures to products, systems and services that are provided by Union controlled entities”. Suppliers from countries with a reciprocal partnership or that belong to the WTO Government Procurement Agreement (GPA) may also bid (draft quoted by Reuters, via Global Times). China is not a GPA party, so Chinese firms are excluded, while US firms can sell computers, simulators and imaging systems to EU governments (SCMP). An EU official added that Chinese-controlled entities in Hong Kong, a GPA member, could be excluded on security grounds (SCMP). Huo Jianguo of the China Society for WTO Studies told Global Times [Chinese state media] the exclusion is “outright discrimination” and that it “will only hold back the EU’s own progress in quantum technology”.

CEAFE comment: Technological and defence dependence is not abstract: it shows up in the number of companies, in spending and in the presence on the ground.

Why it matters for Europe: the Commission’s own figures show the EU hosts 32% of the world’s quantum companies (US 25%, China 5%) but holds just 6% of quantum patents (US 23%, China 46%) (European Commission report published in 2025, cited by GT). Closing the market to China while opening it to the US follows the pattern of the Industrial Accelerator Act and the procurement reform (EPRS). It protects European champions from Chinese competition but not from US competitors. That is a half-sovereignty that matches Washington’s technology-control line.

Key facts

  • Criterion: EU-controlled entities plus GPA/reciprocal partners; security exception case by case.
  • Linked to the Sovereign Tech Equity Fund proposal (covered 25 Nov 20:30).
  • EPRS (Sept 2026) notes the same GPA logic in the Industrial Accelerator Act would exclude China and admit the US.

OPEC+ set to roll over output targets; refining margins slump; Iraq–Kurdistan oil standoff; refinery blast in Bangkok

OPEC+ is likely to roll over its oil output targets for the first quarter, as disputes over capacity persist and the Iran war adds uncertainty (Reuters and Bloomberg, citing sources). Refiners’ margins on turning crude into fuel have slumped to their lowest in months as supply recovers and demand stays sluggish (Bloomberg). In Iraq the federal government and the Kurdish region are in their sharpest confrontation in years over control of oil fields and border crossings, and thousands rallied in Kurdistan (Al Jazeera English). An explosion and fire at the Phra Khanong refinery in Bangkok injured at least seven people (Reuters; Japan Times; Xinhua [Chinese state media]).

Why it matters for Europe: after this morning’s Gulf supply shock (14:30 edition, topic 5), an OPEC+ rollover keeps supply tight while Europe already pays a war premium on LNG. Lower refining margins give some relief on diesel prices.
