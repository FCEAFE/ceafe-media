Reuters: the US used its control of Venezuelan and Iraqi oil money to push both to consider leaving OPEC

The Trump administration encouraged Venezuela and Iraq to consider leaving OPEC earlier this year and stopped Venezuela from paying its annual membership dues, according to ten people familiar with the matter (Reuters exclusive, 27 November). Both are founding members of the cartel. After the US seized Nicolás Maduro in January, the State Department opened talks with Venezuelan officials on leaving OPEC, because Trump wanted US oil majors to revive Venezuelan output without OPEC limits. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman intervened this summer and persuaded Washington not to press further. In Iraq, Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi raised leaving OPEC at a government meeting after talks with US officials, while Exxon Mobil and Chevron were planning big investments there; his oil ministry talked him out of it and suggested asking for a higher quota instead. The leverage is financial: Washington controls Venezuela’s state funds in a US-run account set up after Maduro’s removal, and Iraq’s oil revenues still pass through an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The State Department said leaving OPEC “has not been among the issues raised by high-level officials”. The same morning Trump wrote on Truth Social that he supports al-Zaidi’s efforts to “stop the smuggling of Iraqi oil” and “bring all weapons under the exclusive control of the state”.

Background (CEAFE archive): on 28 August CEAFE shared a Bloomberg report, via The Kobeissi Letter, that Venezuela had been discussing leaving OPEC with US officials (https://x.com/i/status/2093242918030938214).

Why it matters for Europe: this is economic coercion through control of another country’s money, a tool that could be used against any state whose reserves or revenues sit in US accounts. A weaker OPEC would leave oil prices more exposed to US decisions, and Europe imports almost all of its oil.

What to watch: reactions from OPEC, Riyadh, Caracas and Baghdad, which did not respond to Reuters; Iraq’s quota request; the next OPEC+ meeting.

Beijing calls in the state “national team” and Chinese chip stocks post their biggest one-day gain on record

Premier Li Qiang chaired a State Council executive meeting on Friday that called for stronger market-stabilising mechanisms, more dividends and buybacks, a bigger role for state-backed funds in “steadying the market” and a stronger quasi-“stabilization fund” role for the state-owned Central Huijin Investment “to provide sufficient liquidity” (gov.cn, Chinese government; Xinhua, Chinese state media). The readout came out at about 06:00 Madrid time, during trading hours, which is unusual. The Shanghai Composite closed up 3.73%, the Shenzhen Component 7.24% and the STAR board 14.21% (Xinhua). The CSI Semiconductor index of 40 chip stocks jumped 17.6%, its biggest one-day gain on record, led by CXMT, Cambricon and SMIC, and the CSI 300 rose 4%, its biggest gain since October 2024 (CNBC). It was the first time a State Council meeting explicitly referred to the “national team” (analyst Bob Chen, quoted by CNBC).

Background (CEAFE archive): on 20 November CEAFE shared Reuters’ report that US AI-chip curbs had hit Chinese tech stocks and spurred a self-sufficiency push (https://x.com/i/status/2123839200390684841). The 08:30 edition reported that profits in China’s electronics industry doubled in January–October on AI demand.

Why it matters for Europe: Beijing is using state money to back its chip companies on the stock market just as Washington tightens controls on advanced chips. Europe has no comparable tool to back its own semiconductor companies.

What to watch: whether the rally holds next week; any Central Huijin purchases.

Huawei starts selling its Ascend 950 AI chips in South Korea, a US ally

Huawei has started selling the Ascend 950PR (inference) and 950DT (training) chips and the Atlas 950 SuperNode computing system in South Korea, its first sales of Ascend chips in a new foreign market, with partners including SK Shieldus (Huawei Central, citing DigiTimes). According to Huawei’s own figures, the 950PR costs about a quarter of the price of Nvidia’s H20 and gives about 2.87 times its inference performance; these are company claims, not independent tests. South Korea’s market is booming on AI demand: a Reuters poll forecasts that November exports rose 96.8% year on year, the fastest growth since January 1974, and in 1–20 November chip exports rose 294.4% to $38.5bn, 49.7% of all exports (Reuters; Yonhap). A DDR5 16Gb DRAM chip cost $50 in October 2026, against $8.7 a year earlier (Seoul Economic Daily).

Background: Huawei’s US court case and its semiconductor push were covered on 25 and 26 November.

Why it matters for Europe: Huawei now offers a Chinese alternative to Nvidia in a US ally’s market. The AI-chip market is splitting into two blocs, and Europe produces neither leading-edge AI chips nor memory: it buys both, and the memory price boom is raising the cost of its data centres.

What to watch: any US Commerce/BIS or Seoul reaction; full Korean November trade data on 1 December.

Kazakhstan opens its AI market to Russia’s Sberbank and Yandex while buying Nvidia’s newest chips

At Sberbank’s AI Journey conference in Moscow on 26 November, Kazakh President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev told Vladimir Putin that Sberbank and Yandex were welcome in Kazakhstan: “they can rely on our comprehensive assistance” (Reuters). A month earlier, during Nvidia chief Jensen Huang’s visit to Astana, Kazakhstan’s Freedom Holding said it was ordering Nvidia’s latest B300 chips (up to 300 servers) for a planned $2bn “Sovereign AI Hub”. Russia is cut off from US AI chips by sanctions. A new Russian decree lets Russian companies use data centres abroad with the permission of the security services. Reuters did not report any transfer of US chips from Kazakhstan to Russia. Kazakhstan joined the US Pax Silica initiative in June.

Background (CEAFE archive): on 17 November CEAFE shared China’s protest against the new US restrictions on remote access to advanced semiconductors (Global Times, https://x.com/i/status/2122715421203935438), the BIS rule of 16 November aimed precisely at computing power used from third countries.

Why it matters for Europe: EU sanctions on Russia face the same problem with Central Asia. Kazakhstan’s membership of Pax Silica and its opening to Russian AI firms show the limits of US-led technology blocs.

What to watch: BIS or OFAC action on Central Asian data centres; Nvidia B300 delivery terms.

Update: Section 301 tariffs — Japan, India and Vietnam seek their own way out while Brussels has yet to respond

Japan’s trade minister Ryosei Akazawa said on Friday that “we cannot say that there won’t be a significant impact”: the US plan would restore duties on most Japanese goods to 15% from 12.5% and says they could rise to 20% (Japan Times). Indian Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said talks were “close to culmination”; USTR has proposed a 25% Section 301 tariff on India, falling to 17% with a trade deal (Business Standard; Indian Express). Vietnam expects a deal with “a significantly lower final tariff rate” (Bloomberg). The China Council for the Promotion of International Trade urged the US to withdraw the proposed hikes (Global Times, Chinese state media). The European Commission, whose bloc is one of the 14 targets, had not said publicly by 12:40 Madrid time how it will respond. On Euronews, ECIPE’s Hosuk Lee-Makiyama called the tariffs a way of “reconstituting the old tariffs that were necessary to force Europe into a deal”.

Background (CEAFE archive): on 25 November USTR said that “with Section 301, President Trump is going country-by-country” (https://x.com/i/status/2125669000369917960); the 25 November 20:30 edition covered the proposed 15% duty on all EU goods.

Why it matters for Europe: Washington negotiates country by country, and each partner seeks its own exemption. The EU only carries weight if it answers as a bloc; so far it has not said how it will respond. The tariffs are still proposals open to public comment.

What to watch: the Commission’s response and comment filing; a MOFCOM statement.
